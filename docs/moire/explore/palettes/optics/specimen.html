OPTICS1 · Teinte sensible

Under a polarising microscope, the first-order red near 550 nm is the most sensitive tint of the whole interference scale: a few nanometres of shift flip it to blue or to yellow. William Moiré works the same way, small precise interventions with an outsized visible effect. A quiet lilac-grey quartz page carries one red-violet signal, plum ink by day and orchid light by night.

OPTICS2 · Labradorite

Labradorite is a plain grey stone that flashes peacock blue-green when light meets its lamellae, layers thinner than 200 nm interfering inside the crystal. The site is the stone: warm-neutral grey by day, graphite by night, and one petrol flash that keeps exactly the same fill in both modes. Two layers, and a colour that only exists between them.

OPTICS3 · Antireflet

An anti-reflection coating is interference used for comfort: a quarter-wave layer cancels the glare, and all that is left is a faint sea-green sheen with a magenta edge. This palette is built for long reading, with a dimmed grey-card page, a lifted night instead of black, ink near 11.5:1, sea-green actions and the magenta residue kept for focus rings and the moiré. Expertise you feel as the absence of strain.

OPTICS4 · 555 nm

The human eye is most sensitive at 555 nm, a yellow-green: the colour that needs the least light to be seen. William Moiré uses it the way an instrument would, a graphite ground with one luminous signal: a lacquered dark button with a chartreuse label by day, a lit chartreuse button by night. Sharp and exact, and a little whimsical once you know why.

OPTICS5 · Lycurgus

The Lycurgus cup, Roman glass from the 4th century, holds gold and silver nanoparticles: lit from the front it looks jade, lit from behind it glows ruby. One material, two colours, decided only by the relation between light and viewer. Jade-grey glass by day, jade-black glass by night, and a single ruby fill as the constant.
