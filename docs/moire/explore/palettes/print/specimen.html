PRINT1 · Teinte sensible

Pencil graphite on neutral paper, plus one ink: first-order red, the magenta a physicist sees through a gypsum plate under crossed polarisers. It is white light minus green, a colour with no wavelength of its own, and the slightest shift in path difference flips it to blue or yellow. Small shift, large visible change: the moiré promise, as a colour.

PRINT2 · Encre violette

The French school notebook: Seyès paper with its violet lines and blue interlines, written on in the methyl violet ink French classrooms used for a century; the dark side is the carbon sheet underneath. It is where a physicist first wrote equations by hand. The ruling is already a grating: here the moiré is drawn in the blue interline colour, the ink stays violet.

PRINT3 · Vert-de-gris

Copper is what carries the current; left in the air it grows verdigris, the blue-green salt of old domes, roofs and lab fittings. The page is pewter, the accent is the patina, and the moiré lines stay copper underneath. What circulates changes colour and stays the same metal.

PRINT4 · Tableau noir

The physicist's oldest instrument: the green slate board, chalk dust, one stick of lemon chalk for what matters. By night the page is the board; by day the page is paper and the only board left is the button, written on in chalk. Equations are worked out in the open, in front of everyone.

PRINT5 · Deux encres

A risograph prints one drum per ink, and two slightly misregistered screens make the moiré printers fight. Two Riso inks, Plum and Mint, swap roles between modes: plum printed on mint stock by day, mint printed on a plum ground by night, and whichever is the ink is the accent. Two layers, a third pattern, never separate.
